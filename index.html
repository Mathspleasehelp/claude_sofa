      How the model works

      Positions are in centimetres from the sofa's back-left corner. Weights are hand-set guesses grounded in the research below, not fitted to data.

      1. Link every pair

        How well two people can talk depends on how far each has to turn, how far apart they are, whether someone is in the way, and posture.

        wij = √(aij·aji) · e−(d/R)² · sij · √(eiej)

        aij = eκ(cos θ − 1), where θ is how far off their body line the other person sits. Posture sets engagement e (lying 0.55, boxed-in corner 0.80, perched 1.10) and head-turn stiffness κ. Anyone seated within 22 cm of the line between two people cuts the link by up to 55%, or 85% for the boxed-in corner, who can't lean forward to see past.

      2. Rank who holds the floor

        Inside a conversation of k people, your odds of ranking higher grow with how squarely the others face you. All k! speaking orders are summed exactly (Plackett–Luce), and rank m gets this share of the airtime:

        share(m) = rm−1 / (1 + r + … + rk−1)

      3. Score each person's payoff

        Your average link to the group, times a size term that makes bigger groups livelier until a crowd penalty past four kicks in, times diminishing returns on airtime. Being alone is worth 0.06.

        Ui = Āi · (k−1)αe−λ·max(0, k−4) · (½ + ½·E[√(k·share)])

      4. Weigh every split

        Each way of dividing the room is weighted by its total payoff. Splits are set partitions, so there are Bell-number many: 15, 52, 203, 877 and 4,140 for 4 to 8 people. Permutations only come in for speaking order.

        P(split) ∝ exp(Σ Ui / T)

        This is a Boltzmann distribution, so its normaliser is a partition function summed over partitions.

      Grounding: Kendon (1990) on F-formations and the shared o-space a group faces into; Hall (1966) on proxemic distances; Sommer (1959) on people choosing right-angle corner seats for conversation; Steinzor (1950) and Howells & Becker (1962) on seating sightlines deciding who talks and leads; Stephan & Mishler (1952) on geometric decay of participation by rank; Dunbar, Duncan & Nettle (1995) on conversations splitting past four; Luce (1959) and Plackett (1975) on ranking models.
